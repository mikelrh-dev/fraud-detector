Model Evaluation — and a Warning About Perfect Scores

We ran the full documented protocol on a held-out split. The metrics came back perfect. Here is why that is a warning sign, not a victory.

This chapter reports the first published evaluation of the model, following the exact protocol from the training script: stratified 80/20 split (seed 42), the documented feature-noise step and train-only SMOTE, then scoring on the untouched 20% (10,000 transactions). The production artifact was not retrained or modified.

The warning: perfect scores are a red flag

A model that never errs on test data is not a triumph — it is a measurement of how far the synthetic data is from reality. Look at the baselines row: logistic regression scores 0.999. When a linear model matches gradient boosting perfectly, model choice was never the bottleneck. The classes themselves are almost non-overlapping:

This is one of the most important lessons in applied machine learning: evaluation can only be as honest as the data it runs on. These numbers validate that the pipeline is wired correctly — splits, resampling, training, inference — but they say nothing about production performance against real fraud, which is noisy, adaptive and subtly imbalanced. That gap is exactly why the drift monitoring in Chapter 08 exists, and why publishing held-out metrics on real labeled traffic is the top item on the roadmap.

How to read this honestly

Perfect held-out metrics on synthetic data prove engineering correctness, not model quality. Any portfolio claiming F1 = 1.0 as an achievement without this caveat is telling you they did not look closely at their own numbers.

The machinery still had to earn it

Even with separable classes, the cost-sensitive layer was exercised end to end: false negatives priced at 10× false positives, threshold swept from 0.05 to 0.95, economic cost minimized. On easy data the optimal threshold lands low (0.05) because recall is free — on real data this curve becomes the decision-making tool.
